One, ten, a thousand.

You ask an AI agent for one thing. To get it done, the agent hands parts of the job to other agents, and they hand parts on again.

That one request is about to become a lot of work. Here is a small piece of it.

Keep scrolling.

One.

This agent is booking the table. Your agent gave it the job, so you have never spoken to it, and normally you would never see this screen.

It hits a decision only you can make: 9:15 at Nova, or 8:15 at the counter. It sends the question up to the agent that hired it, and waits.

Ten.

Your agent split the birthday into ten jobs: the table, the cake, the guests, a gift, getting Sam from the station. Each one is a conversation like the first.

If you went looking, you would see a title and the last thing each one said.

A hundred.

Each job split again. The cake agent is asking six bakeries at once, and the gift agent is reading forty listings.

Moving means working, blinking means waiting, dim means done. Several are blinking, and only one of them is the table.

A thousand.

Now it is weather. You see patterns where the conversations used to be.

A thousand agents on one birthday sounds absurd. Boris Cherny, who built Claude Code, says that on some days he runs tens of thousands.

Still waiting.

The table agent asked its question 40 minutes ago. It went up to the agent above it, along with a hundred other questions, and reached you as one more notification in the pile.

You never spoke to this agent, so you had no reason to look for it. The 9:15 is gone now.

Somewhere in here is the table agent.

Try to find it. Zoom from a thousand down to one, and open any agent to read what it is doing.

You asked for one thing.

A thousand agents did the work. The one that needed you was a single step from your agent, and its question still arrived looking like everything else.

  • One is a conversation you can follow.
  • Ten is a list you skim.
  • A thousand is weather, and somebody has to design the forecast.
